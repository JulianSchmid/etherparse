# TCP reassembly infers the receiver's state to give up holes and resync

A passive capture misses packets, and a missing segment that the receiver got will never be retransmitted. So the reassembler gives up holes, and treats bytes as ACKed, based on inferred receiver state instead of waiting for a retransmission or an ACK it may never see. There are two inference rules:

- **Implied ACK.** A sender can only send up to ack + window. Bytes seen at offset X therefore prove the receiver ACKed X − window. Holes before X − k×window become overdue, after the same wait for reordering as a hole a seen ACK covers. k defaults to 2 to tolerate window changes, and the window is the largest one the receiver advertised, so a window that shrinks (e.g. to 0 when its buffer is full) doesn't make holes overdue early. If the window is unknown (mid-stream pickup or one-direction capture), a configurable fallback window of 4 MiB is used. The distance is capped at half of the stream's buffer, so that a reader of ACKed bytes keeps up before the buffer is full, but never below a known window, as that would be unsound. So without captured ACKs, such a reader keeps up only if the buffer is clearly larger than the receiver's window; otherwise bytes are rejected and counted.
- **Resync.** When a stream jumps far outside any plausible window without a SYN, it continues at the new position once the other direction's ACKs confirm the jump. Resyncing after N consecutive agreeing segments is opt-in, for one-direction captures.

Without these rules, a stream stalls forever after capture loss or a missed reconnect, and reading only ACKed bytes stalls on one-direction captures. An earlier implementation hit exactly these stalls on real captures.

Giving up is lazy. An overdue hole stays a hole that late segments still fill: one that a seen or the implied ACK covered for a configurable number of packets, and the hole at the read position once the bytes behind it fill half of the buffer (or, for a hole the receiver's ACK shows it lacks, a window past that ACK, leaving 64 KiB of the buffer). It becomes a gap only once the reader reaches it or the connection ends. A rule firing only means the bytes are unlikely to come; giving the hole up right away would drop segments that merely arrive late, e.g. in merged captures, before the reader needs them.

## Why the rules look as they do

The spec states the rules (S3, S4, K2, K4); this is why, and what they replaced.

- **The largest window counts (S4, K2).** The sender can have sent into a larger window before the receiver's window shrank, e.g. to 0 when its buffer is full. With the last window, a lost segment among those in flight made the rest out-of-window and let their ACK resync the stream, and an ACK advertising window 0 made the hole it added overdue at once (user decision, 2026-10-11; council review, 2026-11-07).
- **A SYN's window is unknown under a scale (S3), and the implied ACK waits like a seen ACK (K4(a)).** A SYN's window is never scaled, so under a scale above 0 it is only a lower bound of the window after it. A capture merged from two taps can show the receiver's first scaled window late, and a window that grows later too. Until then, K2 goes by a window that is too small, and a segment reordered behind six later ones became a gap. Round 36 made the implied ACK wait `ack_reorder_tolerance` packets. Round 37 took that out, because the wait counts packets, not bytes, and with large segments one lost segment then overflowed the buffer; it counted a SYN's window as unknown instead. Round 38 showed that a window growing later still needs the wait, which is safe now that K4(d) bounds the stall by the buffer on its own (council review, 2026-11-08, rounds 36 to 38).
- **The implied ACK goes by what the capture showed of the sender (K2).** With the highest offset, which ACKs move, ACKs that a merged capture showed before the bytes made holes that ordinary reordering opened overdue at once (council review, 2026-11-08).
- **The implied ACK's distance (K2)** is at most half of the buffer: with the defaults, k times the fallback window equalled the buffer, so a reader of the ACKed bytes in a one-direction capture sat at the buffer's end, every further segment was rejected, and half the stream was lost. It is never less than a known window, as the sender can have sent that far past the ACK (council review, 2026-11-07). A mid-stream pickup never shows the window scale, so there the fallback window stands in for the window once the capture showed the receiver's ACKs: without that, a pickup with more than half the buffer in flight implied ACKs ahead of the receiver's, and gave up the holes of retransmissions before they came. ACKs that don't count show the receiver's packets too: a pickup that started behind a segment the network lost had the receiver's ACKs before its first byte for a window, and gave a hole up at half the buffer. The stand-in counts at most up to the buffer less 64 KiB: uncapped, a fallback window above the buffer put the implied ACK further back than the buffer, and a reader of the ACKed bytes rejected bytes once the capture lost the receiver's ACKs for a while. A capture of one direction keeps no floor, as its reader of the ACKed bytes would otherwise sit at the buffer's end (council review, 2026-11-08 and 2026-11-09, rounds 39 and 40).
- **K4(b) waits 256 packets by default.** A merged capture shows the ACKs of one direction ahead of the data by the skew between the taps, hundreds of packets per millisecond at 1 Gbit/s; 16 gave captured bytes up as gaps from about 200 Mbit/s on (council review, 2026-11-08).
- **K4(c) gives holes up before rejected bytes.** Rejected bytes at the end of a stream join one hole that each rejected segment extends, so the implied ACK never passes it, and in a continuous flow the reader would stall there (user decision, 2026-10-12).
- **K4(d) bounds every stall by the buffer.** The waits of K2 and K4(b) count windows and packets, not the hole: where a known window kept the implied ACK further back than half the buffer, jumbo frames overflowed a 1 MiB buffer within 256 packets, and in one direction, a hole of two segments plus K2's distance of half the buffer overflowed it too. In each case the bytes after a lost segment were rejected as a second gap. The hole at the read position is overdue once half the buffer is filled from it, checked after every packet and every move of the read position like the other rules: a reader that reached the hole by reading, without a packet after that, stalled. A hole that the receiver's ACK doesn't cover, while its window is known, waits for a window past that ACK instead, up to 64 KiB before the buffer's end: the receiver lacks those bytes, and the sender retransmits them within a window. With half the buffer for every hole, a loss before the tap turned the captured retransmission into a gap once the window was more than half the buffer (council review, 2026-11-08, rounds 37 and 38).
- **The leading data (S4)** mirrors the leading ACK (K1). A receiver's tap that lags past k windows shows the ACKs that would let the window reach the bytes after a burst of capture loss too late. Every later segment lay after the window, and the rest of the stream and its FIN were lost. The bound by the buffer keeps a jump, which lies there by chance only, a resync candidate (council review, 2026-11-08, round 36).

## Considered Options

- **Eager gaps**: giving a hole up as soon as a rule fires is simpler and counts gaps earlier, but drops late bytes the reader hasn't reached yet, and leaves gaps anywhere in the buffer instead of only at the read position.

## Consequences

If the fallback window is set too small, holes are given up whose retransmission would still have arrived. Spoofed ACKs can force a resync, which a passive observer cannot fully prevent, and a spoofed data segment with a FIN after the window plus a spoofed ACK of it end the stream at that FIN, which `force_resync()` doesn't undo. A spoofed SYN or SYN-ACK with a new ISN replaces a live connection as a reconnect (C2), as the reassembler is built for captures without an attacker. All of these are visible in the stream status or the connection's end, so the stream is no longer clean.

Overdue holes keep counting towards the per-stream hole limit until the reader reaches them. A keep-alive's filler byte, sent at the last ACKed byte, could land in an overdue hole the reader hasn't reached, so a 1-byte segment ending at the seen ACK is ignored there.
